About

I'm Louis Belleau.

I'm a psychedelic guide, though most of what I actually do is meditation and conversation.

I grew up in Hong Kong and returned to Canada at fifteen. Since then I've worked between traditions: Zen, yoga, sacred Amazonian plant medicine, cognitive science. I don't think any one of them has the whole answer.

What I offer isn't answers. It's company on the walk back to wholeness.

My Story

They gave me speed
to slow me down

I was thirteen when the Ritalin started. It lasted five years.

It sharpened my focus and dulled everything else. I stopped eating. I stopped laughing. I finally had decent grades, but nothing else got better.

By fifteen I was daydreaming about stepping in front of buses, or jumping off a building. Not because I was mentally ill. Because I was sensitive, and paying attention to a sick culture.

The life on offer was a 9 to 5, debt, and endless consumerism. It seemed terrible to me. At seventeen I took mushrooms, reconnected to spirit, and wanted to live again.

Then I chased the mystical dragon. Bigger visions, more often. I integrated nothing. The dark night of the soul arrived on a high dose of LSD and took everything apart.

What put me back together wasn't more. It was less. Multiple ten-day silent retreats. A decade of sitting. Breathwork. And later, ayahuasca. As a teacher, not a thrill.

The point of this work isn't transcendence. It's return.

Background

I studied Psychology, Philosophy, and Comparative Religion at McGill, then earned an MA in Psychedelics and Consciousness Studies at the University of Ottawa. I founded Psygaia, a psychedelic and ecological medicine nonprofit. I've been practicing Zen and Vipassana for over a decade. Dzogchen now.

None of this is theoretical. I teach what actually worked.

The Work

The oldest question,
and a path through it

Why does the well-lived life look so different from the one being sold to us?

That gap has been named for as long as people have been writing things down. The Buddha called it dukkha. Ecclesiastes called it vanity, a chasing after wind. Aristotle separated hedonic pleasure from eudaimonia, genuine flourishing. Many indigenous traditions never located meaning inside the individual at all, but in relationship: with community, with land, with the living world.

And there's money in keeping the gap open. A hungry person is a reliable consumer.

Seeing the gap is one thing. Closing it is another. Inquiry, meditation and breathwork change how you relate to your own mind and body. Psychedelics are relational medicines: they widen that to everything you are part of.

Inquiry. Examining the assumptions the thinking mind takes for granted. Meeting the intellect on its own ground and inviting it to see through itself.

Meditation. Two capacities, trained deliberately. Collectedness: attention that stays where you put it. Decentering: watching a thought without standing inside it.

Breathwork. Taking the work into the body. It regulates the nervous system and returns awareness to the intelligence the body already carries.

Psychedelics. A direct encounter with what you've been avoiding. I prepare you, sit with you in person, and integrate afterwards. Or I train you to work on your own. An accelerant, not a shortcut.

What Changes

The destination is surprisingly ordinary

Not some elevated spiritual state. Something far more livable. None of it is dramatic, and that's the point. You're not becoming someone else. You're getting back to yourself.

Aliveness

The flatness lifts. Not euphoria, but something gentler and more sustainable. You're engaged with your own life again.

Direction

Clarity emerges. Not a five-year plan. A felt sense of what matters and what to move toward.

Embodiment

Less in your head, more in your body, your relationships, your actual life. The distance between head and heart begins to close.

Belonging

Less alone in it. More connected: to people, to the living world, to something larger than yourself.

Authenticity

The performed version of you, built for other people's expectations, loosens its grip. What remains is more honest.

Meaning

A felt sense of purpose that doesn't depend on achievement. A reason to be here you actually believe in.

Who This Is For

You might recognize yourself

The person burning out or breaking down. You've run hard for years and something's giving out. What looks like breakdown is often a deeper intelligence asserting itself.

The person in the wake of a psychedelic or spiritual experience. Something opened, and you're still living in the gap between what you now know and the life you returned to.

The person navigating a dark night. The structures that once organized your sense of meaning have collapsed. It resembles depression but reaches further.

The person living with depression, anxiety, or addiction. Often symptoms of a deeper disconnection: from the body, from meaning, from the living world. I work with the whole context, not the diagnosis.

Therapy often explores the past. Coaching tends to focus on strategy, behaviour, and what you want to change. Underneath both is something contemporary cognitive science calls predictive processing: the brain constantly uses past experience to predict what is happening now, which shapes what you notice and how you respond. When reality doesn't match those predictions, the brain can update its expectations. But old patterns stay surprisingly persistent, even when you understand them intellectually. Meditation, breathwork and psychedelics may create different conditions for noticing and revising those patterns, through changes in attention, arousal and perception. They don't replace reflection or behaviour change. They can sometimes open a route that insight alone doesn't.
